Acknowledgement of Country

Research the place, recognise its people, and connect your words to your event.

Sources checked 30 September 2026. This guide supports your research; it does not determine cultural authority for a particular venue.

Acknowledgement or Welcome?

An Acknowledgement recognises Traditional Custodians and their continuing relationship with Country. Anyone can give one. It contributes to reconciliation through recognition and respect, especially when supported by ongoing relationships and action.

A Welcome to Country is delivered by Traditional Owners, or Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people authorised by them. Writing an Acknowledgement does not authorise an organiser to deliver a Welcome. Arrange a Welcome with appropriate local representatives.

Source: Reconciliation Australia — protocols and meaning

Identify Country for your venue

  1. Record the exact venue address. A capital-city label alone is not enough.
  2. Use the AIATSIS map and its explanation for orientation. Its group locations are general, boundaries are not fixed, and some information is contested. It is not a precise address lookup.
  3. Follow the relevant local source below to find a Traditional Owner organisation, Aboriginal Land Council or local council Indigenous engagement contact. Ask who can advise on that address, preferred names and pronunciation.
  4. Keep the source URL and date you checked it. Where sources disagree, seek local guidance; do not choose a name simply to complete the form. Use the Generic option while researching if appropriate.

Examples of direct contacts include Whadjuk Aboriginal Corporation and the Larrakia Nation directory entry. Sydney's council guide below identifies the Metropolitan Local Aboriginal Land Council as a contact. These organisations advise within their own areas, not across all Australia.

Make your Enhanced statement meaningful

Use these writing prompts in the event form. They are suggestions for reflection, not a script or cultural approval.

  1. Place and people: Who have you verified as the Custodians of this venue? Record the name and research source.
  2. Respect: Recognise continuing connections to Country and pay respect to Elders using locally appropriate wording.
  3. Your event: What have you genuinely learned about this place? Explain its relevance to gathering, food or community without inventing traditions or speaking for others.
  4. Action: Name a realistic commitment, such as engaging a First Nations supplier on agreed terms or following locally advised care for Country. Only promise what you will do.

Read your draft aloud, check names and pronunciation, and review it before publication. The form checks that text and a source are supplied; it cannot verify cultural accuracy or endorsement.

Common mistakes and contested issues

  • Tokenism: avoid copying a statement without understanding the place or following through on commitments.
  • False certainty: maps, spellings and institutional acknowledgements can differ. Ask locally rather than presenting a contested identification as settled.
  • Confusing protocols: do not describe your own Acknowledgement as a Welcome without the relevant authority.
  • Erasing living cultures: recognise continuing connections and contemporary communities, not only the past.
  • Using knowledge without permission: do not invent or reproduce restricted stories, language, artwork or ceremonies. Seek permission and agree on payment for requested cultural expertise.

This checklist applies the protocol and map cautions above to event planning. Local cultural advice takes priority over generic wording.
